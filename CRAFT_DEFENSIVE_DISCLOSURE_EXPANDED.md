# 1. Purpose of this disclosure

CRAFT is a family of methods for evaluating a person's cognitive
familiarity with a text by testing access to lexical, structural, and
sentence-level compositional decisions associated with that text.

The central premise is that authorship can be evaluated not only by
analyzing properties of a finished textual artifact, but also by
eliciting evidence from the purported author concerning decisions
embodied in that artifact. A person who participated substantially in
planning, drafting, lexical selection, sentence formulation,
organization, and revision is expected to possess cognitive familiarity
with at least some of those decisions. A person presented with text
generated by another person, a large language model (LLM), or another
external source may understand the text while lacking the same access to
the particular compositional decisions that produced it.

CRAFT therefore treats authorship verification as an elicitation
problem:

1.  obtain or identify a reference text;
2.  derive individualized test material from that text;
3.  alter, remove, replace, insert, paraphrase, fabricate, or otherwise
    manipulate selected textual elements;
4.  ask the purported author to recognize, identify, classify, or
    restore those elements; and
5.  use the resulting performance, optionally including accuracy,
    response time, behavioral information, or combinations thereof, as
    evidence concerning familiarity with the compositional history of
    the text.

This disclosure documents three principal CRAFT embodiments developed
sequentially:

-   **CRAFT Version 1:** paper-based structural and lexical restoration;
-   **CRAFT Version 2:** paper-based sentence classification using
    Original, Modified, and Fabricated sentence types; and
-   **CRAFT Version 3 / CRAFT-RT:** computerized response-time CRAFT
    using sequential Original, Modified, and Fabricated sentence
    classification and server-authoritative behavioral measurement.

The three versions are embodiments of the same broader concept rather
than mutually exclusive systems. Features from one version may be
combined with features from another.

# 4. Core CRAFT principle

## 4.1 Authorship as a cognitive relationship

CRAFT distinguishes between:

-   **artifact-centered inference**, in which authorship is inferred
    from statistical properties of the final text; and
-   **author-centered elicitation**, in which evidence is elicited from
    the purported author about the text.

The CRAFT hypothesis is that composing and revising a text can create
retrievable traces of decisions concerning wording, sentence
construction, organization, and other compositional properties.
Consequently, a genuine author may possess information unavailable from
the final artifact alone.

This does not require perfect episodic recall. CRAFT may exploit graded
familiarity, recognition, restoration ability, discrimination speed, or
combinations of these signals.

## 4.2 Individualized challenge construction

A CRAFT test is derived from a particular reference text. The challenge
is therefore individualized to the text whose authorship or cognitive
engagement is being assessed.

Possible operations include:

-   insertion of plausible sentences;
-   deletion of original material;
-   replacement of lexical items;
-   synonym substitution;
-   paraphrasing;
-   syntactic restructuring;
-   controlled modification of original sentences;
-   generation of semantically plausible but non-original sentences;
-   presentation of genuine original sentences;
-   reordering;
-   restoration tasks;
-   classification tasks; and
-   combinations of the above.

## 4.3 Surface plausibility

Manipulated material should preferably remain plausible enough that the
task cannot be solved reliably through trivial grammatical, semantic,
lexical, formatting, positional, or stylistic anomalies.

This is a central design property. The intended signal is familiarity
with compositional decisions, not merely the ability to detect badly
generated distractors.

# 5. CRAFT Version 1 --- structural and lexical restoration

## 5.1 Overview

Version 1 combines two individualized restoration components:

1.  **structural restoration through sentence-intruder detection**; and
2.  **lexical restoration through synonym detection and
    reconstruction**.

The reference implementation accepts a student's text, generates
manipulated test material, produces a printable individualized test, and
produces an answer key.

## 5.2 Structural restoration

The source text is segmented into sentences. Plausible intruder
sentences are generated for insertion into the text.

The implementation aims to prevent intruders from being identifiable
through superficial irregularities. Among other things, the generation
and placement procedures can consider:

-   topical and local coherence;
-   sentence length;
-   lexical/content density;
-   similarity to existing sentences;
-   duplication;
-   contextual insertion position;
-   distribution of intruders across the paragraph;
-   irregularity of spacing; and
-   estimated detectability of the inserted sentences.

The reference implementation generally targets four intruders when
source length permits. Shorter texts may use fewer.

Intruder placement is deliberately non-mechanical. Candidate placements
can be evaluated for dispersion and for whether an automated detector
can identify the inserted sentences. Candidate paragraphs can be
regenerated or repositioned when intruders appear too easily detectable.

The resulting text is presented with numbered sentences. The respondent
identifies sentences believed not to have appeared in the original text.

### 5.2.1 Structural scoring

Correct intruder identifications receive positive credit. Incorrect
identification of an original sentence as an intruder can receive a
negative penalty.

The penalty discourages a strategy of selecting large numbers of
sentences indiscriminately.

Alternative embodiments may use:

-   different positive/negative weights;
-   probabilistic scoring;
-   confidence-weighted scoring;
-   item-response models;
-   response-time scoring;
-   no penalty;
-   adaptive penalties; or
-   empirically calibrated decision thresholds.

## 5.3 Lexical restoration

Selected lexical items from the reference text are replaced by
contextually plausible synonyms.

The implementation attempts to make replacements difficult to
distinguish through trivial lexical properties. Candidate replacements
may be constrained by:

-   semantic appropriateness;
-   part of speech;
-   morphological form;
-   grammatical function;
-   corpus-frequency rank or frequency band;
-   avoidance of identical stems;
-   edit distance from the original and from other words;
-   uniqueness within the text;
-   avoidance of unusually obscure or salient alternatives; and
-   contextual plausibility.

The respondent performs two related acts:

1.  identifies which visible words are replacements; and
2.  restores the corresponding original lexical items.

Hints such as the first letter and part of speech of an original word
may be supplied to constrain the response space.

### 5.3.1 Lexical scoring

The Version 1 study awarded credit for successful restoration of
original lexical choices, including morphologically related forms where
the semantic root was preserved. Incorrect lexical responses were not
penalized in the same manner as false intruder selections.

Alternative embodiments may require exact restoration, lemma
restoration, semantic equivalence, multiple-choice recognition,
confidence judgments, or response-time measurement.

## 5.4 Combined evidence

Structural and lexical components can be scored separately or combined.

The original empirical implementation treated structural familiarity and
lexical familiarity as complementary evidence concerning prior
compositional engagement.

# 6. CRAFT Version 2 --- Original / Modified / Fabricated sentence classification

## 6.1 Overview

Version 2 changes the surface form of CRAFT while preserving its central
construct.

Instead of asking the respondent to restore individual lexical items and
identify inserted sentences in a continuous paragraph, Version 2
constructs a set containing three conceptual sentence classes:

-   **Original (O):** a sentence from the reference text;
-   **Modified (/ or M):** a meaning-preserving transformation of an
    original sentence; and
-   **Fabricated (X or F):** a plausible sentence not present in the
    original text.

The respondent classifies presented sentences according to these
categories.

Terminology and symbols may vary without changing the method.

## 6.2 Original sentences

An Original item preserves the wording of a source sentence.

A system may retain some source sentences exactly while transforming
others. Selection can be random, stratified, balanced, position-aware,
or optimized according to item characteristics.

## 6.3 Modified sentences

Modified sentences preserve the intended meaning of an original sentence
while altering its surface realization.

The reference implementation generates multiple paraphrase candidates at
differing degrees of lexical overlap. Candidates are screened for
semantic similarity and lexical perturbation.

### 6.3.1 Semantic-equivalence screening

The implementation uses sentence embeddings to evaluate semantic
similarity between the source sentence and a candidate transformation.

The presently implemented threshold is:

-   semantic similarity \>= **0.80**.

This value is a tunable implementation parameter and is not fundamental
to CRAFT.

Other semantic-equivalence measures, models, human review, entailment
systems, or combinations may be substituted.

### 6.3.2 PINC-based perturbation

The implementation calculates a PINC-like lexical perturbation measure
from n-gram non-overlap between the original and candidate sentence.

For n-gram orders represented in the candidate, the system calculates
the proportion of candidate n-grams not shared with the source and
averages those values.

The current reference implementation includes tunable values:

-   `PINC_O_MAX = 0.20`
-   `PINC_SLASH_MIN = 0.30`
-   `PINC_SLASH_MAX = 0.60`
-   `INTRUDER_PINC_MIN = 0.80`

These numbers describe the current implementation only. They are
explicitly **not essential fixed boundaries of the CRAFT method**.

Modified candidates are preferentially selected from a target PINC band.
The implementation also contains controlled fallback ranges when
insufficient candidates satisfy the strict band.

Candidate selection can deliberately distribute accepted modified items
across the permitted perturbation range rather than clustering them at
one point.

## 6.4 Fabricated sentences

Fabricated sentences are generated to fit locally within the reference
text while not reproducing an original or modified sentence.

Generation may use neighboring sentence context so that the fabricated
item:

-   is topically plausible;
-   fits the discourse locally;
-   is grammatically natural;
-   resembles the surrounding text in length and density; and
-   does not merely announce itself as an outlier.

The reference implementation additionally tests fabricated candidates
against original and modified sentences using PINC constraints. The
present code prefers high perturbation relative to reference sentences
and may relax generation constraints in controlled stages if necessary.

A fabricated item therefore need not be semantically unrelated to the
source. Indeed, topical plausibility is desirable.

## 6.5 Deliberate overlap among item classes

CRAFT does not require Original, Modified, and Fabricated items to
occupy perfectly separable distributions on machine-observable
similarity metrics.

In some embodiments, overlap is desirable.

For example:

-   a subtle Modified sentence may be highly similar to an Original
    sentence;
-   an aggressively Modified sentence may resemble a Fabricated sentence
    on lexical-distance measures; and
-   a locally plausible Fabricated sentence may resemble the surrounding
    text more closely than some Modified sentences.

The respondent's prior relationship with the source text is therefore
intended to supply information beyond a simple surface-similarity
threshold.

## 6.6 Tunable and variable perturbation ranges

PINC thresholds and related generation constraints are implementation
parameters.

They may be:

-   fixed;
-   manually configured;
-   selected from predefined profiles;
-   randomized within validated bounds;
-   varied between administrations;
-   varied between test-generation sessions;
-   varied by item;
-   empirically calibrated;
-   adapted to text characteristics; or
-   deterministically derived from a private or administrator-held
    value.

One contemplated embodiment records a test-generation start time, such
as a Unix timestamp. A deterministic transformation of that recorded
value may select a perturbation band or generation profile from a
predetermined acceptable region. This allows later reconstruction of the
generation parameters while avoiding a permanently fixed operational
cutoff.

A further embodiment combines the timestamp with an administrator-held
secret or other private seed. This additional secret is optional and
need not be implemented for timestamp recording itself to be useful.

The generation timestamp may initially be recorded solely as metadata
and later be used as an input to variable item-generation parameters.

## 6.7 Why threshold variability can also be methodological

Variable perturbation ranges are not solely an adversarial measure.

They permit empirical examination of whether CRAFT discrimination
generalizes across degrees of sentence modification and can support
analysis of perturbation magnitude as:

-   an item-level predictor;
-   an item-difficulty parameter;
-   a moderator of response accuracy;
-   a moderator of response time; or
-   a component of adaptive test construction.

Accordingly, variation can be randomized or balanced within the same
population or period to avoid confounding perturbation profile with
cohort, calendar time, or practice.

# 7. CRAFT Version 3 --- CRAFT-RT

## 7.1 Overview

CRAFT-RT converts the Version 2 sentence-classification concept into a
computerized response-time assessment.

A reference text remains visible while individualized test items are
presented sequentially. For each item, the respondent classifies the
sentence using three fixed response categories corresponding to:

1.  Original;
2.  Modified; and
3.  Fabricated.

The principal extension is that CRAFT-RT measures not merely whether the
respondent eventually identifies an item correctly, but also the speed
and behavioral process of that response.

## 7.2 Sequential item release

Items are randomized once for a test instance and the resulting
presentation order is persisted.

Only the currently released question needs to be sent to the client.
Future items and answer keys can remain server-side.

Sequential release has several functions:

-   establishes an item-specific response interval;
-   prevents simple inspection of all future questions;
-   permits per-item behavioral recording;
-   allows interruption/recovery logic; and
-   supports server-authoritative state.

## 7.3 Response-time measurement

CRAFT-RT records response time for each item.

The current architecture retains both:

-   client-side response-time information for diagnostic/research
    purposes; and
-   server-authoritative elapsed time for scoring.

Server timing begins when a question view is released and is not reset
merely by reloading the browser.

The system may preserve first-exposure or first-completed timing as the
canonical item timing even when the respondent subsequently revisits or
changes an answer.

Alternative embodiments may use client timing, server timing,
synchronized timing, hardware timing, or other latency measurements.

## 7.4 Error penalties

A response-time score can incorporate a penalty for an incorrect
classification.

One implemented scoring rule is:

`adjusted response time = scoring response time + error penalty`

with an example error penalty of **5,000 ms** for an incorrect answer.

The penalty value is tunable.

Other embodiments may use:

-   different fixed penalties;
-   category-specific penalties;
-   adaptive penalties;
-   nonlinear penalties;
-   confidence-adjusted penalties;
-   omission penalties;
-   total-score transformations; or
-   joint statistical models of accuracy and latency.

The combination of speed and correctness discourages an automated or
human respondent from obtaining an artificially low score merely by
answering very rapidly without adequate classification accuracy.

## 7.5 Navigation and revisits

CRAFT-RT may permit navigation to previous items.

A revisit can create a new view record while preserving the canonical
first-exposure timing used for the primary item score.

The system can therefore distinguish:

-   initial response behavior;
-   later review;
-   changed responses; and
-   final submitted responses.

Reloads, navigation origins, visibility changes, and answer changes can
be stored as separate behavioral variables.

## 7.6 Scrolling behavior

The reference text may be displayed in a scrollable panel.

CRAFT-RT can record scroll bursts associated with each item view,
including variables such as:

-   event start and end;
-   duration;
-   starting scroll position;
-   ending scroll position; and
-   distance traveled.

Scrolling need not itself determine the CRAFT score. It may instead
serve as behavioral evidence useful for:

-   construct research;
-   anomaly detection;
-   identifying unusual response processes;
-   distinguishing normal text consultation from automated answering; or
-   validating remote administrations.

An absence of scrolling across a completed test may be treated as a
potential anomaly where the display and text length ordinarily require
or strongly elicit scrolling. Such a signal should preferably be
empirically calibrated rather than treated as conclusive proof of
automation.

## 7.7 Interruption and reload recovery

An unfinished item view can remain authoritative across reloads or
interruptions.

Reloading does not necessarily create a fresh scoring interval. This
prevents trivial timer reset by refreshing the page.

Closed views can be prevented from being replayed as if they were still
active.

## 7.8 Persistent server state

A computerized implementation may maintain authoritative test state in a
database, including:

-   test instance;
-   item identities;
-   randomized presentation order;
-   release times;
-   responses;
-   correctness;
-   client and server RT;
-   scoring RT;
-   timing source;
-   penalties;
-   adjusted RT;
-   view histories;
-   navigation;
-   visibility events;
-   reload counts; and
-   scrolling events.

The current implementation uses a server-side database and private
test-input/result storage, but CRAFT is not dependent on a particular
cloud provider, database, programming language, or hosting platform.

## 7.9 Known-text and unknown-text baselines

CRAFT-RT may be administered with calibration conditions.

A **known/self-authored-text baseline** estimates the participant's
performance when genuine authorship familiarity is expected.

An **unknown-text baseline** estimates performance when the participant
lacks the relevant compositional history.

Later CRAFT performance can then be interpreted relative to
within-person baselines rather than solely against population
thresholds.

This permits analysis of:

-   median or mean RT;
-   RT distribution;
-   variability;
-   accuracy;
-   adjusted RT;
-   sentence-length effects;
-   scrolling;
-   revisits;
-   navigation;
-   and other behavioral characteristics.

A later test that is implausibly faster than the same person's
known-text baseline may be considered anomalous, subject to empirical
calibration for practice and other legitimate effects.

## 7.10 Remote and in-person administration

CRAFT-RT can be administered:

-   remotely on an unmanaged device;
-   remotely in a controlled browser;
-   in a supervised laboratory;
-   in a classroom;
-   on institutionally controlled computers;
-   in a kiosk or locked-down environment; or
-   in other authenticated testing environments.

In-person or controlled-device administration provides stronger control
over identity and client-side automation. Remote administration provides
greater practical accessibility but cannot completely prevent
sophisticated local automation or identity substitution.

# 8. Adversarial properties and optional anti-automation mechanisms

## 8.1 Operating the interface versus solving the test

Automating button presses is distinct from correctly solving CRAFT.

An automated system attempting to obtain a favorable CRAFT-RT score may
need to infer the correct category for each item while lacking the
purported author's compositional familiarity.

For Version 2/3, this can require distinguishing:

-   Original from subtly Modified;
-   Modified from Fabricated; and
-   Original from Fabricated.

If Modified and Fabricated distributions overlap on surface similarity
measures, identifying that an item is merely "not original" is
insufficient.

## 8.2 Hidden or changing operational parameters

Public source code need not imply permanently fixed operational
thresholds.

A public implementation may expose the general PINC-selection method
while allowing administrators to configure or vary the live ranges.

Possible parameter-selection inputs include:

-   administrator configuration;
-   generation-session timestamp;
-   random seed;
-   private seed;
-   test identifier;
-   predefined profile;
-   text properties; or
-   combinations thereof.

## 8.3 Error-cost amplification

Where incorrect answers incur response-time penalties, uncertainty in an
attacker's classification procedure can increase the resulting score.

For example, under a 5,000-ms penalty, three additional classification
errors add 15,000 ms to aggregate adjusted response time.

The precise penalty should be empirically justified for substantive use;
the broader disclosed method is the joint use of latency and accuracy
such that classification errors impose a scoring cost.

## 8.4 Behavioral anomaly evidence

Optional signals include:

-   zero or unusually little scrolling;
-   implausibly uniform response times;
-   implausibly fast response times;
-   unusual relationships between sentence length and RT;
-   anomalous navigation;
-   repeated visibility changes;
-   divergence from personal known-text baselines;
-   absence of expected text consultation;
-   and multivariate combinations of these features.

These can be used as flags rather than automatic determinations.

## 8.5 CAPTCHA-like property

CRAFT may be viewed as a familiarity-based challenge in which the
legitimate respondent possesses privileged cognitive information arising
from a prior relationship with the stimulus.

Unlike a conventional CAPTCHA, the objective need not be simply to
distinguish a human from a machine. It may instead distinguish a
particular expected author/familiar respondent from an entity that has
access only to the presented artifact.

This is an optional interpretation of the method, not a requirement of
CRAFT.

## 9.8 Generalization from text to authored artifacts

The CRAFT principle is not inherently linguistic.

A broader formulation is:

> obtain an artifact attributed to a particular creator; identify
> recoverable or perturbable production characteristics associated with
> creation of that artifact; generate authentic, altered, omitted,
> reordered, or fabricated alternatives; and measure whether the
> purported creator can recognize or restore the characteristics that
> actually occurred during production.

The relevant "compositional decisions" can therefore be understood as
**production decisions** more generally.

A production decision may be visible in the finished artifact, encoded
in project metadata, recoverable from an associated project file, or
derivable from a version history or creation environment. The
assessment may use only the final artifact or may use an artifact
together with legitimately available production metadata.

The following multimodal and non-text embodiments are expressly
contemplated.

### 9.8.1 Source code and software artifacts

For source code, scripts, notebooks, configuration files, database
queries, markup, or software projects, CRAFT items may manipulate or ask
the creator to restore:

-   variable, function, class, module, or file names;
-   indentation, whitespace, formatting, or brace conventions;
-   quote style;
-   comments and comment placement;
-   control-flow structures;
-   algorithm selection;
-   data structures;
-   API or library choices;
-   dependency versions;
-   regular expressions;
-   command-line arguments;
-   error-handling strategies;
-   constants or thresholds;
-   type annotations;
-   query structure;
-   schema decisions;
-   function decomposition;
-   refactoring choices;
-   loop versus vectorized implementations;
-   recursion versus iteration;
-   specific debugging workarounds;
-   platform-specific conditionals;
-   naming conventions;
-   test cases;
-   assertion style;
-   log messages;
-   commit-message language;
-   order of implementation steps; or
-   other programmer-specific or project-specific decisions.

An item can preserve behavior while changing implementation. For
example, a Modified code item may be functionally equivalent to the
original but use different variable names, library calls, control flow,
or abstractions. A Fabricated code item may be plausible within the
project but never have appeared in the creator's implementation.

A code-oriented CRAFT test may therefore distinguish:

-   exact original code;
-   behaviorally equivalent but modified code;
-   plausible code that fits the project but was not written; and
-   code from another author or generated system.

Dynamic equivalence can be checked using unit tests, static analysis,
symbolic execution, type checking, sandboxed execution, or human review.

### 9.8.2 Photographs and still images

For photographs, image-editing projects, illustrations, or rendered
images, restorable production characteristics may include:

-   camera body or lens choice;
-   focal length;
-   aperture / f-stop;
-   shutter speed;
-   ISO;
-   exposure compensation;
-   white balance;
-   focus point or focus mode;
-   flash use;
-   orientation;
-   aspect ratio;
-   crop choice;
-   capture sequence;
-   bracket selection;
-   filter choice;
-   color profile;
-   lighting arrangement;
-   subject position;
-   camera position or angle;
-   depth-of-field choice;
-   layer structure;
-   masking;
-   selection boundaries;
-   retouching operations;
-   local adjustments;
-   curves or levels;
-   color-grading choices;
-   sharpening;
-   noise reduction;
-   compositing decisions;
-   brush/tool selection;
-   export format;
-   compression level; or
-   other capture/edit decisions.

A test need not ask factual trivia such as "what is ISO?" Instead it may
present the actual setting among plausible altered settings, ask the

Where EXIF or project metadata exists, the system may use it to
construct answer keys while withholding the answer from the test taker.
Where metadata is absent, production characteristics may be inferred
from source project files, edit history, captured variants, or creator

## 9.9 Artifact-state and production-state CRAFT

A useful general distinction is between:

-   **artifact-state CRAFT**, where all challenge information is derivable
    from the finished artifact itself; and
-   **production-state CRAFT**, where challenges may additionally use
    legitimately retained metadata, source/project files, revision
    histories, intermediate states, or device/application settings.

Artifact-state CRAFT is maximally portable because only the final
submission is required.

Production-state CRAFT can test richer process characteristics and may
be substantially harder to reconstruct after the fact, but it depends
on availability and integrity of production records.

Hybrid systems can use both.

## 9.10 Hidden-feature restoration

-   a CAD feature parameter;

-   a spreadsheet formula; or
-   a version-control branch or sequence of edits.

The test may therefore assess familiarity with hidden process state,
provided the answer key derives from a trustworthy production record.

## 9.12 Counterfactual and near-neighbor generation

For any artifact type, a particularly useful Modified item is a

-   acoustic output;
-   analytical result;

## 9.14 Partial, collaborative, and distributed creation

For multimodal artifacts, authorship may be role-specific.

CRAFT can estimate familiarity separately for:

-   writing;
-   photography;

-   coding;
-   statistical analysis;
-   sound;
-   design;
-   animation;
-   production;
-   revision; or

A collaborator need not be expected to recognize decisions made by

## 9.15 Human-AI co-creation

In AI-assisted workflows, the relevant question may be neither "human"
nor "AI" authorship.

CRAFT can instead estimate the creator's familiarity with decisions
across the artifact.

Potential conditions include:

-   human-generated and human-edited;
-   AI-generated but heavily human-selected and revised;
-   AI-generated with minimal human review;
-   human-generated with AI copyediting;
-   AI-generated code integrated and debugged by a programmer;
-   AI-generated images selected, masked, composited, or retouched by a
    designer;
-   generative video edited by a human;
-   AI-generated music arranged or mixed by a human; and
-   mixed human/AI scientific or analytical workflows.

This makes CRAFT potentially useful as a measure of **cognitive or
production engagement**, not only binary authorship.

## 10.1 Automated/stylometric authorship and AI-text detection

A large literature attempts to classify a finished text using
statistical or linguistic properties such as token probabilities,
perplexity, stylometry, neural representations, or generation-time
watermarks.

These systems analyze the artifact. CRAFT instead generates a
personalized challenge from the artifact and elicits evidence from the
purported author.

The Version 1 CRAFT preprint reviews this literature and explicitly
frames CRAFT as a process-oriented alternative to artifact-centered
probabilistic classification.

**Distinction:** artifact classification is not the same as testing the
purported author's ability to recognize or restore prior compositional
decisions.

## 10.6 Recognition, concealed-information, and familiarity paradigms

Psychological and forensic paradigms have long examined differential
responses to familiar versus unfamiliar stimuli. Concealed-information
and recognition approaches can use accuracy, latency, physiological
response, or other measures to infer whether information is known to a
respondent.

**Relevance:** These paradigms make familiarity/recognition and response
latency known general concepts.

**Distinction:** CRAFT applies the principle to individualized authored
text by algorithmically constructing alternatives around the writer's
own lexical and structural decisions. The relevant "known information"
is not simply a fact or previously memorized secret; it is the
compositional realization of a submitted artifact.

## 10.7 Response-time authentication and CAPTCHA systems

Prior systems have used response latency as part of human verification,
liveness testing, behavioral authentication, and CAPTCHA-like systems.

**Relevance:** response time itself is not claimed here as a newly
invented measurement.

**Distinction:** CRAFT-RT couples response latency to personalized
text-restoration/classification items derived from a purportedly
authored artifact. In one embodiment, errors are converted into an
explicit time cost and performance can be interpreted against the
respondent's own known-text/unknown-text baselines.

## 10.8 Cognitive and semantic CAPTCHA approaches

Prior CAPTCHA research and patents have proposed challenges based on
semantic understanding, language processing, object recognition,
personal knowledge, or other cognitive tasks rather than
distorted-character recognition.

**Relevance:** cognitive challenge construction and human/machine
asymmetry are established ideas.

**Distinction:** CRAFT is not primarily based on a generic human
cognitive advantage. The intended advantage is person/text-specific:
familiarity arising from the respondent's expected prior participation
in producing the particular artifact.

## 10.9 Cloze-test authorship verification

**Foltýnek, T., Kancko, T., & Rychly, P. (2025), "Authorship
Verification Using Cloze Test with Large Language Models."**

ACL Anthology:

https://aclanthology.org/2025.ranlp-1.45/

This work is particularly relevant because it explicitly proposes
verifying document authorship by asking a purported author to complete a
cloze test derived from the document. The authors report that a
10-item cloze-test classifier achieved accuracy of 0.988 and an
F1 score of 0.937 in their reported setting.

**Relevance:** Like CRAFT, the method converts a submitted document into
an individualized post-hoc test and uses the purported author's
performance rather than only the artifact's statistical properties.

**Distinction:** A conventional cloze item removes material and asks the
respondent to supply a missing element. CRAFT Version 1 additionally
requires discrimination between genuine and deliberately substituted
lexical choices and detection of plausible inserted structural material.
CRAFT Version 2 explicitly constructs Original, Modified, and Fabricated
classes, including meaning-preserving perturbations whose purpose is to
test familiarity with the exact compositional realization rather than
merely the ability to predict a contextually likely completion.
CRAFT-RT further incorporates response latency, error cost, persistent
first-exposure timing, navigation and scrolling behavior, and
within-person familiarity baselines.

The cloze-test work therefore represents close neighboring prior art and
also illustrates why CRAFT should not be defined merely as "generating a
test from a document." The narrower CRAFT concept is manipulation or
restoration of production choices in a way designed to distinguish
artifact comprehension or contextual predictability from familiarity
with the artifact's actual compositional history.

## 10.10 Testing understanding of selected words and phrases

**US 11,853,708 B1, "Detecting AI-generated text by measuring the
asserted author's understanding of selected words and/or phrases in the
text," Robert A. Connor.**

Google Patents:

https://patents.google.com/patent/US11853708B1/en

A related continuation-in-part is:

**US 2024/0378382 A1, "Method for Detecting AI-Generated Text by
Measuring the Asserted Author's Understanding of Selected Words and/or
Phrases in the Text."**

Google Patents:

https://patents.google.com/patent/US20240378382A1/en

The earlier patent describes automatically selecting words or phrases
from submitted text, particularly relatively rare or advanced items,
and generating a test of the asserted author's understanding. One

**Relevance:** This work, like CRAFT, generates a personalized
assessment from the submitted text and uses a human response to infer

**Distinction:** The central construct is semantic knowledge: a writer

structure, organization, or other production decisions of the artifact.

actually chosen, which sentence was actually present, which alternative
is a controlled modification, or which production setting generated a
particular artifact.

The use of synonyms in both systems is therefore technically adjacent
but functionally different. A synonym can be used to test whether a
respondent knows a word's meaning, or it can be used as a perturbation
against which the respondent must restore or recognize the creator's

**US 2025/0141857 A1, "System and Method for Detecting and Verifying

Wong, Andrew J. Thompson, and Lee J. Lorenzen.**

Justia:

https://patents.justia.com/patent/20250141857

The disclosed pipeline receives submitted material, performs an
AI/human detection analysis, flags material according to a score, then
prompts the creator for a response to a question or task and evaluates
that response against the submitted material to produce a verification
score. The disclosure also states that analogous detection/verification
pipelines can be extended beyond text to image, audio, video, and other

**Relevance:** This is important neighboring art because it combines
artifact analysis with a later challenge directed to the asserted
creator and because it expressly contemplates non-text media.

**Distinction:** The located disclosure is framed broadly around a
verification question or task following an AI-detection stage.
CRAFT does not require an initial AI detector or flagging threshold.
Its challenge is itself the principal measurement and is generated by
systematically preserving, deleting, altering, substituting, inserting,
reordering, or fabricating features of the particular artifact or its
production characteristics. CRAFT further contemplates explicit
authentic/modified/fabricated discrimination, controlled perturbation
distance, creator-specific restoration, and latency/behavioral evidence.

Accordingly, CRAFT can be used even when the system makes no prior claim
that an artifact is AI-generated.

## 10.12 Concealed-information testing and familiarity detection

The Concealed Information Test (CIT), Guilty Knowledge Test, recognition
paradigms, and related forensic-memory methods provide an important
conceptual predecessor to familiarity-based CRAFT measurements.

A CIT generally embeds a critical or familiar item among plausible
irrelevant items and infers recognition from differential responses.
Depending on the implementation, these responses may include skin
conductance, respiration, EEG/P300, eye movements, or reaction time.

A particularly relevant patent is:

**US 11,020,034 B2, "Concealed information testing using gaze
dynamics."**

Google Patents:

https://patents.google.com/patent/US11020034B2/en

The patent describes, among other embodiments, presenting familiar and
unfamiliar objects, measuring gaze behavior, and using differences in
response speed and/or accuracy on a short-term-memory prompt as evidence
that particular objects are familiar.

Relevant reaction-time CIT research includes work showing that familiar
probe items can produce systematically different response-time patterns
from irrelevant items, as well as research on stimulus similarity,
familiarity, filler items, response conflict, and countermeasures.

Examples include:

-   Georgiadou, K., Chronos, A., Verschuere, B., & Sauerland, M.
    Reaction time-based concealed-information testing in eyewitness
    identification.
-   Lukács, G., & Ansorge, U. Work on familiarity-related fillers and
    response-time CIT.
-   klein Selle, N., Or, B., Van der Cruyssen, I., Verschuere, B.,
    Ben-Shakhar, G., and colleagues on response conflict in
    reaction-time concealed-information detection.
-   Kohn Lukic, L. Z., Möck, N., Verschuere, B., & Sauerland, M. Work
    on target familiarity in reaction-time face recognition.

**Relevance:** CIT establishes that familiarity with a particular
stimulus can be inferred from differential behavioral or physiological
responses and that plausible comparison stimuli, timing, and repeated
trials can be used for memory detection.

**Distinction:** CRAFT creates the relevant familiar/unfamiliar or
authentic/manipulated alternatives from an artifact attributed to the
respondent. The critical information is not merely an externally
designated crime fact, known face, or memorized object. It consists of
production decisions encoded in a purportedly created artifact.
Moreover, Modified items can occupy an intentionally intermediate state:
they derive from the genuine artifact and preserve content while
altering the original realization.

This intermediate class is potentially important because it permits
testing not merely "known versus unknown" but discrimination among
"actually produced," "derived from what was produced," and "plausible
but not produced."

## 10.13 Response-time CAPTCHA, liveness, and risk scoring

**US 2020/0097643 A1 / US 11,663,307 B2, "rtCaptcha: A Real-Time Captcha
Based Liveness Detection System."**

Google Patents:

https://patents.google.com/patent/US20200097643A1/en

This system uses the time taken to solve a CAPTCHA or other challenge as
part of a liveness/authentication procedure and can combine timing with

**US 10,250,629 B2, "Captcha risk or score techniques."**

Google Patents:

https://patents.google.com/patent/US10250629B2/en

This disclosure uses CAPTCHA correctness together with response duration
or expected response-time ranges to estimate whether a client may be
solving the challenge autonomously and to calculate a risk or score.

**Relevance:** These references demonstrate that timing a challenge,
using implausibly fast or slow completion as an anomaly signal, and
combining correctness with timing were known authentication concepts.

**Distinction:** CRAFT-RT does not rely on a generic human-versus-bot
task. It attempts to create an asymmetric challenge whose legitimate
solver has person-specific prior familiarity with the source artifact.
The relevant baseline may therefore be the same person's response to
known/self-created and unknown/non-created artifacts, rather than a
generic human timing threshold.

CRAFT's optional error-to-time conversion also serves a different
purpose from a simple maximum or minimum CAPTCHA timing threshold. It
creates a joint speed/accuracy quantity so that an automated classifier
cannot improve its apparent score solely by responding rapidly while
accepting substantial classification error.

## 10.14 Behavioral biometrics and continuous authentication

A large literature and patent landscape exists for behavioral
authentication using typing cadence, mouse movement, touch gestures,
cursor trajectories, navigation, gaze, voice characteristics, device
interaction, and other user-specific signals.

These methods are relevant to CRAFT-RT's optional recording of
scrolling, navigation, visibility, revisits, timing variability, or
other interaction behavior.

**Distinction:** Such signals can supplement CRAFT, but they do not
define it. CRAFT can operate with accuracy-only paper testing and does
not require prior biometric enrollment. Conversely, behavioral signals
can be fused with CRAFT performance to create a stronger
multi-evidence verification model.

## 10.15 Conventional oral defense, viva, and follow-up questioning

Educational practice has long used oral examinations, vivas, defenses,
and instructor questioning to assess whether a student can explain or
defend submitted work.

**Relevance:** These practices recognize that interacting with the
purported author can reveal evidence unavailable from the final
submission alone.

**Distinction:** Ordinary oral defense is generally open-ended and tests
knowledge, reasoning, or comprehension. CRAFT automatically or
semi-automatically derives controlled challenges from specific
production decisions in the artifact, allowing standardized scoring,
repeatable item construction, explicit manipulation classes, and
fine-grained behavioral measurement.

CRAFT can nevertheless be combined with oral defense. For example, a
system may first identify an anomalous CRAFT item and then ask the
respondent to explain the corresponding compositional decision.

## 10.16 General cloze, recognition, and memory-restoration testing

Cloze procedures, recognition memory tests, source-memory tasks,
old/new recognition paradigms, forced-choice recognition, cued recall,
and reconstruction tasks are longstanding assessment and cognitive
methods.

**Relevance:** Removing information and asking a person to restore it,
or presenting an old item among alternatives and asking whether it was
previously encountered, are not in themselves new operations.

**Distinction:** CRAFT links those operations specifically to a claimed
creator/artifact relationship and intentionally generates plausible
alternative realizations of that person's purported production
decisions. The response is used as evidence concerning production
familiarity or authorship rather than merely memory capacity, vocabulary
knowledge, or comprehension.

## 10.17 Code authorship, software stylometry, and developer biometrics

Prior research in source-code authorship attribution and software
forensics has used features such as identifier naming, formatting,
syntax-tree patterns, API usage, whitespace, comments, structural
complexity, coding idioms, and machine-learning representations to infer
which programmer likely wrote a code sample.

Other systems capture developer behavior during composition, including
keystrokes or development-environment activity.

**Relevance:** These methods establish that source code contains
creator-associated stylistic and behavioral features.

**Distinction:** A code-oriented CRAFT embodiment need not infer the
programmer from static style. Instead it can challenge the asserted
developer to restore or recognize project-specific implementation
decisions: which identifier, API, control structure, abstraction,
algorithm, data representation, error-handling strategy, comment,
dependency, constant, or unusual workaround actually appeared in the
creator's code.

Thus static code stylometry and code CRAFT are complementary:
one predicts authorship from the artifact, while the other elicits
creator-specific evidence about the artifact.

## 10.18 Digital provenance, content credentials, watermarks, and
metadata

Digital provenance systems can record where a file came from, who or
what signed it, which application produced it, and what transformations
occurred. Examples include cryptographic signatures, secure provenance
logs, content credentials, media authenticity standards, generation
watermarks, file metadata, revision histories, and version-control
records.

**Relevance:** These systems also seek evidence concerning origin and
production history.

**Distinction:** Provenance records are machine-stored evidence attached
to or associated with an artifact. CRAFT elicits evidence from the
purported creator's cognition or behavior. Either source can exist
without the other.

A future CRAFT implementation may deliberately combine them. Production
metadata may provide additional restorable features, or CRAFT results
may be stored alongside cryptographic provenance records as a separate
human-familiarity signal.

## 10.19 Similarity, paraphrase, and perturbation metrics

PINC, BLEU-like n-gram overlap, edit distance, semantic textual
similarity, embeddings, entailment, syntactic similarity, lexical
frequency, paraphrase detection, style transfer, and adversarial
perturbation methods all predate CRAFT.

**Relevance:** These methods can be used to generate or select
manipulated items.

**Distinction:** CRAFT does not treat a similarity metric itself as
proof of authorship. Similarity is an item-construction variable. The
measurement comes from the purported creator's differential response to
the resulting artifact-specific alternatives.

## 10.20 Multimodal AI detection and creator verification

Systems for detecting synthetic images, audio, and video use forensic
features, model fingerprints, watermarking, metadata, sensor noise,
compression traces, spectral properties, and learned classifiers.
Creator-verification systems may also request an additional action,
recording, or challenge.

**Relevance:** These systems share the broad goal of verifying the
origin of non-text media and show that the human/AI-authenticity problem
is not limited to language.

**Distinction:** A multimodal CRAFT embodiment specifically converts
production choices associated with the claimed creator into a
restoration or recognition challenge. It therefore tests the
creator/artifact relationship rather than merely whether an artifact
contains statistical traces of synthesis.

## 10.21 Why the neighboring art matters

The closest neighboring concepts can be arranged along several axes:

| Neighboring approach | Primary evidence | Typical question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stylometry / AI detection | final artifact | "What produced this artifact?" |
| Writing-process monitoring | recorded production behavior | "How was this artifact produced?" |
| Comprehension testing | semantic knowledge | "Does this person understand it?" |
| Cloze authorship verification | ability to fill document-derived gaps | "Can this person reconstruct missing content?" |
| CIT / familiarity testing | differential response to known vs unknown stimuli | "Does this person recognize this information?" |
| CAPTCHA / liveness | challenge correctness and/or timing | "Is this a live human rather than an automated process?" |
| Provenance / signatures | stored machine evidence | "What system or identity is recorded as the origin?" |
| **CRAFT** | artifact-specific restoration, discrimination, latency, and behavior | **"Does this purported creator show the expected familiarity with the particular decisions that produced this artifact?"** |

CRAFT can incorporate tools from any of the neighboring approaches while
remaining conceptually distinct from each one.

# 12. Distinguishing CRAFT from comprehension testing

Comprehension and authorship familiarity are related but separable.

A non-author may read a submitted text carefully and subsequently answer
questions about:

-   its topic;
-   factual content;
-   argument;
-   examples; or
-   conclusions.

Such performance demonstrates comprehension but does not necessarily
demonstrate participation in the decisions that produced the exact
wording and organization.

CRAFT instead manipulates those decisions themselves.

Examples include:

-   whether a particular lexical item was the author's original choice;
-   whether a sentence actually occurred in the source;
-   whether a semantically equivalent sentence preserves meaning but
    changes the author's wording;
-   whether a plausible sentence was never present at all; and
-   how rapidly the respondent discriminates among these alternatives.

Accordingly, a respondent can understand every proposition in a document
while still performing differently from its author on CRAFT.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 13. Distinguishing CRAFT from stylometric verification

Stylometric verification generally asks:

> Does this artifact statistically resemble artifacts associated with
> this writer or class of writers?

CRAFT asks:

> Does this respondent demonstrate the expected familiarity with the
> compositional decisions embodied in this particular artifact?

The former can often be performed without contacting the purported
author. The latter intentionally elicits new evidence from that person.

CRAFT therefore does not require the existence of a large historical
writing corpus for the purported author.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 14. Distinguishing CRAFT from writing-process surveillance

Keystroke logging, revision histories, screen recording, and behavioral
biometrics can provide strong evidence about how a text was produced,
but they require instrumentation during production.

CRAFT can be generated after the fact from a finished artifact.

This makes it applicable where:

-   the original writing environment was not instrumented;
-   a student submits an ordinary document;
-   privacy or infrastructure constraints make continuous surveillance
    undesirable; or
-   an instructor wishes to verify cognitive engagement after
    submission.

CRAFT and process logging can also be combined.

# 15. Example generalized CRAFT workflow

A generalized implementation may perform the following:

1.  receive a reference artifact associated with a purported author;
2.  segment the artifact into candidate units;
3.  select one or more units for manipulation;
4.  generate one or more altered or fabricated alternatives;
5.  evaluate alternatives for semantic, lexical, structural, stylistic,
    or contextual plausibility;
6.  retain candidates meeting configurable acceptance criteria;
7.  construct an individualized test;
8.  randomize presentation;
9.  present the challenge to the purported author;
10. collect classifications, restorations, response times, confidence,
    navigation, or other behavior;
11. score the responses;
12. compare performance with population or individual reference
    distributions; and
13. report evidence relevant to authorship familiarity.

Not every embodiment must perform every step.

# 16. Example CRAFT-RT scoring workflow

For item (i):

-   let (RT_i) denote the authoritative response time;
-   let (E_i = 1) when the response is incorrect and (0) otherwise;
-   let (P) denote an error penalty.

A simple adjusted latency is:

\[ ART_i = RT_i + P E_i \]

and a test-level score may be:

\[ CRAFT`\text{-}`{=tex}RT = `\sum`{=tex}\_i ART_i \]

or an average, median, trimmed statistic, normalized statistic,
model-derived estimate, or other aggregation.

The presently implemented 5,000-ms penalty is one example rather than a
limitation of the method.

A scoring model may additionally condition on:

-   item type;
-   PINC;
-   semantic similarity;
-   sentence length;
-   item position;
-   prior exposure;
-   participant baseline;
-   scrolling;
-   revisits; or
-   other item/person variables.

## 17.1 Multimodal validation

The same validation logic used for text can be applied to other
artifact types.

Potential experiments include:

-   self-shot versus externally supplied photographs;
-   self-edited versus externally edited photographs;
-   self-recorded versus externally supplied video;
-   self-edited versus externally edited video;
-   self-written versus externally generated code;
-   self-produced versus externally produced audio;
-   self-built versus externally supplied CAD models;
-   self-created versus externally supplied spreadsheets;
-   self-analyzed versus externally analyzed datasets; and
-   fully self-created versus partially assisted multimodal projects.

Within-person designs are especially attractive because each participant
can serve as their own known-artifact and unknown-artifact comparison.

## 17.2 Production-parameter memory

Future research can estimate which production decisions leave the
strongest recoverable familiarity traces.

For photography, for example, a creator may remember composition and
lens choice much better than an exact shutter speed.

For programming, a developer may strongly remember an unusual
workaround or API choice but poorly remember whitespace.

For video editing, clip choice and cut location may be more memorable
than codec settings.

Accordingly, item difficulty can be empirically calibrated by parameter
type rather than assumed.

## 17.3 Deliberate versus incidental decisions

Production characteristics can be divided into:

-   deliberate decisions;
-   habitual conventions;
-   defaults accepted without attention;
-   automatically selected settings; and
-   post-production transformations performed by software.

CRAFT may work best for decisions involving conscious selection or
meaningful interaction.

This distinction can be measured empirically and used to weight items.

## 17.4 Salience and distinctiveness

A production decision may be more memorable when it is:

-   unusual;
-   effortful;
-   revised repeatedly;
-   consequential;
-   consciously debated;
-   associated with an error or debugging episode;
-   visually or semantically salient;
-   selected from multiple alternatives; or
-   connected to a creator's stable convention.

An item-generation system can estimate such salience from revision
history, edit count, dwell time, version history, commit history, or
other production traces.

## 17.5 Revision-history CRAFT

Where revision history is available, challenges can be generated not
only from the final artifact but from rejected alternatives.

Examples:

-   "Which of these sentences was your earlier version?"
-   "Which variable name did you replace?"
-   "Which image crop preceded the final crop?"
-   "Which clip was removed?"
-   "Which model specification did you try first?"
-   "Which CAD dimension was changed?"
-   "Which EQ setting was reduced?"

Rejected creator-generated alternatives may be particularly valuable
distractors because they are highly plausible yet have a genuine
relationship to the production process.

They also permit research on different kinds of memory:

-   final-state familiarity;
-   earlier-state familiarity;
-   rejected-option familiarity; and
-   fabricated-option unfamiliarity.

## 17.6 Difficulty calibration and item response theory

Items can be calibrated using:

-   classical difficulty and discrimination;
-   item response theory;
-   Rasch-family models;
-   hierarchical accuracy/RT models;
-   diffusion models;
-   survival/time-to-response models;
-   Bayesian familiarity models;
-   person-specific z-scores;
-   anomaly scores; or
-   machine-learning classifiers trained on validated behavioral data.

Difficulty predictors may include perturbation distance, semantic
similarity, visual similarity, acoustic similarity, functional
equivalence, item salience, source position, revision frequency, and
time since creation.

## 17.7 Confidence and metacognition

Respondents can report confidence in each classification or restoration.

Confidence can be analyzed jointly with accuracy and RT.

A genuine creator may exhibit not only higher accuracy but a different
relationship among confidence, speed, and correctness.

Possible scores include:

-   confidence-weighted accuracy;
-   calibration error;
-   high-confidence error rate;
-   confidence/RT interaction;
-   confidence change on revisit; and
-   creator-specific confidence baselines.

## 17.8 Mouse, touch, gaze, and interaction trajectories

Beyond scrolling, CRAFT may optionally record:

-   cursor movement;
-   hover duration;
-   click trajectory;
-   touch gestures;
-   touch pressure where available;
-   eye gaze;
-   fixation;
-   pupil response;
-   page zoom;
-   text selection;
-   copy events;
-   focus changes;
-   keyboard navigation; and
-   window visibility.

These signals can be used for research or anomaly detection but need not
be essential to scoring.

## 17.9 Physiological CRAFT

A controlled laboratory version may combine artifact-derived challenges
with:

-   eye tracking;
-   pupil dilation;
-   EEG/ERP;
-   skin conductance;
-   heart-rate variability;
-   facial response;
-   voice latency; or
-   other psychophysiological measurements.

This embodiment connects CRAFT with concealed-information methodology

## 17.10 Adaptive test construction

An adaptive CRAFT system may choose the next item based on posterior
uncertainty about familiarity.

For example:

1.  begin with moderately difficult Original/Modified/Fabricated items;
2.  estimate the respondent's current familiarity profile;
3.  select more diagnostic item types or perturbation levels;
4.  stop when a target confidence or evidence threshold is reached.

Adaptive generation can reduce test length while preserving diagnostic
information.

## 17.11 Dynamic perturbation calibration

Instead of fixed PINC or similarity bands, the system may estimate a
local neighborhood around each artifact unit.

For text, this could mean finding sentence-specific paraphrase distances
that humans judge equally plausible.

For images, it could mean generating crops or edits of matched visual
distance.

For code, it could mean functionally equivalent refactorings of matched
complexity.

For video, it could mean edits differing by matched numbers of frames or
matched perceptual distance.

The principle is to calibrate **item alternatives relative to the local
artifact neighborhood** rather than apply one global threshold.

## 17.12 Generative adversarial item construction

A generator and detector/critic may be used iteratively:

1.  generate a manipulated or fabricated candidate;
2.  test whether a generic model can distinguish it from authentic
    material using trivial cues;
3.  reject overly obvious candidates;
4.  regenerate or revise;
5.  retain candidates that remain plausible yet differ in production
    history.

Human judges can be included in the critic loop.

This generalizes Version 1's detectability-aware intruder generation to
all artifact types.

## 17.13 Ensemble item generators

Different generation mechanisms may create independent item families.

For text:

-   rule-based substitution;
-   LLM paraphrase;
-   syntactic transformation;
-   human editing.

For code:

-   AST transformation;
-   formatter-based changes;
-   compiler-preserving refactoring;
-   LLM rewriting;
-   human refactoring.

For images:

-   metadata perturbation;
-   crop/transform generation;
-   conventional editing;
-   generative fill;
-   alternative color grade.

Using heterogeneous generators may reduce vulnerability to a single
reverse-engineered item-generation signature.

## 17.14 Secret, public, and committed parameterization

Operational parameters may be:

-   fully public;
-   administrator-configured;
-   randomly generated;
-   derived from a timestamp;
-   derived from a test identifier;
-   derived from a secret seed;
-   derived from a cryptographically secure random value; or
-   combinations thereof.

A system may publish the general algorithm while keeping an operational
seed private.

A further embodiment commits to the seed or parameter profile before
test administration using a cryptographic hash, then reveals it later
for auditability. This can demonstrate that parameters were not changed
after observing the respondent's performance.

## 17.15 Challenge pools and one-time items

A reference artifact may generate more valid items than are needed for a
single test.

The system may create a large pool and release only a randomized subset.

Items may be:

-   one-time use;
-   retired after exposure;
-   recycled after a washout period;
-   regenerated from the same source unit;
-   selected adaptively; or
-   partitioned among repeated administrations.

This reduces the value of sharing a complete answer key.

## 17.16 Cross-session memory decay

CRAFT can measure familiarity as a function of delay after creation.

Potential intervals include:

-   immediate;
-   hours;
-   days;
-   weeks;
-   months; and
-   longer archival periods.

Decay curves may differ by production decision type.

Within-person longitudinal calibration could distinguish normal memory
decay from anomalously low familiarity.

## 17.17 Practice effects

Repeated CRAFT administration can itself create familiarity with the
test items.

Possible countermeasures include:

-   fresh item generation;
-   unrepeated source units;
-   alternative perturbations;
-   large challenge pools;
-   separate calibration items; and
-   explicit statistical modeling of exposure count.

## 17.18 Accessibility and individual differences

Response time can vary for reasons unrelated to authorship, including
language proficiency, reading speed, motor ability, device, disability,
age, fatigue, attention, and testing environment.

Accordingly, CRAFT can use:

-   accuracy-only modes;
-   generous timing;
-   within-person baselines;
-   alternative response modalities;
-   accessibility accommodations; and
-   scoring models that separate motor latency from familiarity effects.

## 17.19 Device and network normalization

Server-authoritative timing can contain network or rendering components.

Future implementations can estimate or control:

-   network latency;
-   page-render latency;
-   device performance;
-   browser event timing;
-   server clock precision;
-   visibility interruptions; and
-   offline intervals.

A hybrid timing model can preserve server integrity while estimating the
participant-facing stimulus interval more precisely.

CRAFT-RT scoring need not rely on a mean.

Possible summaries include:

-   median;
-   trimmed mean;
-   winsorized mean;
-   quantiles;
-   log RT;
-   inverse efficiency;
-   diffusion-model parameters;
-   hierarchical latent scores;
-   robust z-scores;
-   mixture models; and

## 17.21 Evidence combination

CRAFT can generate separate evidence dimensions:

-   restoration accuracy;
-   classification accuracy;
-   adjusted RT;
-   raw RT;
-   confidence;
-   scrolling;
-   revisits;
-   gaze;

## 17.22 Section-level and region-level authorship

Instead of assigning one score to an entire artifact, CRAFT can localize
familiarity.

For text, each paragraph or sentence cluster can receive a familiarity
estimate.

For code, each function/module can be tested.

For images, different layers or regions can be tested.

For video, different clips or sequences can be tested.

For a collaborative artifact, this can reveal a plausible map of which
parts were actually produced or substantially edited by which
contributor.

## 17.23 Provenance-assisted challenge generation

When production logs are available, a system can preferentially select
items with stronger ground truth.

Examples:

-   a word known to have been revised three times;
-   code with a documented refactor;
-   an image with multiple saved crop versions;
-   a video clip with alternate takes;
-   a spreadsheet formula with version history;
-   a CAD feature with recorded parameter changes.

The answer key can be anchored to actual production records rather than
generated retrospectively.

## 17.24 Privacy-preserving CRAFT

Because source artifacts may be confidential, item generation may occur:

-   locally on the creator's device;
-   on institution-controlled infrastructure;
-   within a trusted execution environment;
-   using encrypted storage;
-   using temporary processing followed by deletion;
-   by sending only selected features to an external model; or
-   through hybrid local/cloud processing.

A CRAFT score can be exported without permanently storing the entire
artifact.

## 17.25 Auditability and reproducibility

For consequential use, systems may retain:

-   source-artifact hash;
-   generation timestamp;
-   generator/model version;
-   prompt or transformation version;
-   random seed;
-   item-generation parameters;
-   candidate rejection history;
-   accepted items;
-   answer key;
-   test version;
-   response timestamps;
-   scoring-code version; and
-   final score.

This permits later reconstruction of how the test was produced.

## 17.26 Human review of generated challenges

Automated generation can produce invalid items.

A review interface may let an instructor or administrator:

-   reject ambiguous items;
-   approve semantic equivalence;
-   verify fabricated-item plausibility;
-   inspect hidden metadata;
-   adjust difficulty;
-   flag sensitive information; and
-   freeze a final test version.

Human review and automated generation are complementary.

Potential attacker conditions include:

-   generic LLM with only the test item;
-   LLM with the full reference artifact;
-   LLM with the item-generation source code;
-   LLM with estimated PINC thresholds;
-   agent with browser automation;
-   classifier trained on prior CRAFT tests;
-   human non-author who studies the artifact;
-   human proxy test taker;
-   collaborative attacker;
-   model with access to production metadata; and
-   model with leaked prior answer keys.

The relevant question is not whether automation is impossible, but what

## 17.28 Cognitive-CAPTCHA research

The CAPTCHA analogy can be tested directly.

Possible metrics include:

-   attack success rate;
-   legitimate-user success rate;
-   time cost per attempt;
-   number of required model calls;
-   sensitivity to hidden generation parameters;
-   benefit from access to the reference artifact;
-   benefit from access to source code;
-   effect of one-time item generation;
-   error-penalty amplification; and
-   transfer across users or artifact types.

The distinctive security resource is expected to be **creator-specific

## 17.29 Detecting post-hoc study of an artifact

A difficult non-author may read or study the artifact before taking
CRAFT.

Research can compare:

-   genuine creator;
-   naive non-author;
-   non-author after brief reading;
-   non-author after extended study;
-   non-author allowed to take notes;
-   non-author given the artifact during the test; and
-   creator after comparable delay.

Such experiments directly test whether compositional familiarity remains

## 17.30 Measuring degrees of contribution

CRAFT scores may be modeled continuously rather than as pass/fail.

A contributor who:

-   drafted the entire artifact;
-   revised it substantially;
-   lightly copyedited it;
-   selected among generated alternatives; or
-   merely submitted it

may show different familiarity profiles.

This is particularly relevant to human-AI collaboration and team
production.

## 17.31 Artifact-independent calibration tasks

A test may include generic calibration trials measuring:

-   motor response speed;
-   reading speed;
-   visual discrimination;
-   memory span;
-   device latency; and
-   response-key familiarity.

These can help isolate artifact-specific familiarity from general

## 17.32 Cross-artifact creator baselines

A person may complete CRAFT on multiple unquestionably self-created
artifacts.

This can establish a personal distribution of:

-   accuracy;
-   RT;
-   confidence;
-   consultation behavior;
-   restoration success;
-   memory decay; and
-   modality-specific performance.

A disputed artifact can then be compared with the same creator's normal
familiarity pattern.

## 17.33 Cross-language and translation CRAFT

For multilingual artifacts, challenges can distinguish:

-   original wording;
-   translated wording;
-   machine-translated wording;
-   human post-editing;
-   paraphrased translation; and
-   independently composed target-language text.

A translator may possess strong familiarity with semantic and lexical
decisions in the target text even when not responsible for the source
text.

CRAFT can therefore model role-specific authorship rather than treat
translation as simple non-authorship.

## 17.34 Accessibility-preserving alternative modalities

The same underlying challenge can be delivered through:

-   visual text;
-   text-to-speech;
-   spoken response;
-   keyboard;
-   switch input;
-   touch;
-   eye gaze; or
-   assistive technology.

The construct should remain familiarity with production decisions, not
speed of operating a particular interface.

## 17.35 High-stakes safeguards

Where CRAFT is used for consequential decisions, a score should be
treated as evidence rather than an infallible verdict.

Possible safeguards include:

-   manual review;
-   retesting;
-   alternative assessment;
-   confidence intervals;
-   minimum reliability requirements;
-   calibrated false-positive targets;
-   documented accommodations;
-   appeal procedures; and
-   combining CRAFT with independent evidence.

These safeguards do not alter the technical method but are important
for responsible deployment.

# 20. Public CRAFT Version 1 empirical disclosure

The Version 1 preprint reports an experiment with 60 undergraduate
English-education majors completing CRAFT tasks derived from both
self-authored handwritten texts and LLM-generated texts on the same
topics.

The Version 1 instrument combined sentence-intruder structural
restoration and synonym-based lexical restoration.

The reported results showed substantially higher performance for
self-authored material, with the preprint reporting a total-score paired
effect size of Cohen's (d = 5.34). The empirical results are not
necessary to define CRAFT, but they provide an early validation of the
underlying familiarity-based assessment principle.

# 22. Concluding disclosure

CRAFT originated as a process-based method for testing whether a
purported author can restore lexical and structural properties of a
particular text. It subsequently developed into a three-class
sentence-recognition/restoration method and then into a response-time
system.

Across these embodiments, the common inventive concept is the use of a
**personalized challenge derived from the purportedly authored or
created artifact, and optionally from trustworthy associated production
state, to elicit evidence of the respondent's cognitive familiarity
with production decisions embodied in or associated with that
artifact**.

Although CRAFT originated in writing, "production decisions" are
expressly intended to include non-linguistic creation decisions such as
source-code implementation choices, camera settings, image-editing
operations, frame rate, shot and cut selection, audio-production
settings, CAD feature histories, spreadsheet formulas, statistical
workflows, design choices, and other recoverable characteristics of
creating an artifact.

The disclosed implementations demonstrate multiple ways of
operationalizing that concept, including lexical restoration, structural
intruder detection, Original/Modified/Fabricated sentence
classification, controlled semantic-preserving perturbation, fabricated
contextual alternatives, response latency, accuracy penalties,
behavioral measurements, and individualized baselines.

The specific implementation details can evolve while preserving this
underlying method.
